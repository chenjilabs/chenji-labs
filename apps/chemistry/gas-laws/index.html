Gas laws

Aim

Aim: find out how the pressure, volume and temperature of a gas are related, by holding one of them constant at a time.

Particles are illustrative and not to scale.

Law to investigate

Pressure 0 kPa

Results

No readings yet.

Conditions

  • The gas is ideal: its particles take up no space and do not attract each other.
  • The amount of gas stays fixed unless you change it.
  • Temperature is in kelvin (K = °C + 273.15).

What to try

  1. Choose Boyle's law. Record readings at 10 L, 20 L and 40 L. What happens to the pressure when the volume doubles? Which column of the table stays the same?

    The pressure halves each time the volume doubles. PV stays the same: at constant temperature, pressure is inversely proportional to volume.

  2. Choose Charles's law. Record readings at 200 K, 300 K and 400 K. Which column stays the same?

    V/T stays the same: at constant pressure, volume is proportional to the kelvin temperature.

  3. Choose Gay-Lussac's law and double the temperature from 200 K to 400 K. What happens to the pressure?

    The pressure doubles: at constant volume, pressure is proportional to the kelvin temperature. The particles move faster and hit the walls harder and more often.

  4. Double the amount of gas in any mode. What changes?

    With twice as many particles, the computed quantity doubles: the pressure in Boyle's and Gay-Lussac's modes, or the volume in Charles's mode.

Check your understanding

A gas in a sealed, rigid container is heated from 300 K to 600 K. What happens to its pressure?

  • At constant volume and amount, pressure is proportional to the kelvin temperature, and the temperature has doubled.

  • Heating makes the particles move faster, so they hit the walls harder: the pressure rises, not falls.

  • The container is rigid, so the volume is fixed, but the pressure still changes with temperature.

  • Pressure is proportional to T, not to T². Particle speed goes as √T, but the pressure goes as T.

Why must temperature be in kelvin when you use these laws?

  • The laws are proportionalities, so the temperature scale must start at zero where the gas would have no thermal motion. That is the kelvin scale.

  • The size of the numbers doesn't make them more accurate. Doubling 10 °C to 20 °C does not double the volume, because 10 °C is not half as hot as 20 °C.

  • Celsius temperatures can be negative, for example −10 °C. The real reason is that the laws need a scale that starts at absolute zero.

Science behind this

PV = nRT

R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. With P in kilopascals and V in litres the equation works directly, because 1 kPa × 1 L = 1 J. Holding two of the four quantities fixed gives each law: Boyle's (PV constant), Charles's (V/T constant) and Gay-Lussac's (P/T constant).

Assumptions

  • The pressure shown is computed from PV = nRT. It is not counted from particle collisions.
  • The drawn particles are illustrative and not to scale: their number and speed only suggest what the gas is doing.
  • The gas is ideal, the amount is fixed unless you change it, and temperatures are in kelvin.

Limits

Real gases deviate from the ideal gas law at high pressure and low temperature.
